# 400 Matrix: Sampling and Inference Confusables

Sources: Ch 2 notes sheet (Oct 24), Ch 3 and 4 notes sheet (Oct 24), miss log.
Traps marked "miss log" are Ben's actual misses. Use cue first, formula second.

| Concept | What it is | Formula or skeleton from notes | Question cue that triggers it | Classic trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Population mean | The true middle of the whole group. The centre the sampling distribution is centred on. Usually unknown. | Y ~ N(mean, SD^2); first slot is the mean | "What is the sampling distribution centred at?" | Miss log: answered sample standard deviation. Correct centre is the population mean. |
| Sample mean (Y-bar) | The middle of the small part actually measured. A statistic used to guess the true mean. | Add all values, divide by N | "From this sample, estimate the mean" | Treating one sample mean as the exact true mean. Big samples get close (Law of Large Numbers), small samples wobble. |
| SD (s for a sample) | How spread out single values are. Typical distance of values from the mean. | Sample SD: divide by N minus 1, not N. In N(4, 9), SD is sqrt(9) = 3, because the second slot is variance. | A question about ONE value uses SD | Miss log: read N(4, 9) as SD 9. Take the square root of the second slot. Also miss log: the sample SD recipe itself needed a scaffold on the Oct 26 quiz. |
| SE | How spread out sample means are, if many samples were taken. | SE = s / sqrt(N). Bigger N means smaller SE. | A question about THE MEAN uses SE | Miss log: chose 28 (the SD) as the typical distance of a sample mean. Correct: 28 / sqrt(49) = 28 / 7 = 4. |
| Point prediction | One best guess for the next single value. | Point prediction = mean. Always. | "Guess the next single value, one guess only" | Giving a range when one guess was asked, or guessing anything other than the mean. Not in held notes beyond this rule: verify for other settings. |
| Prediction interval (Ch 2 form) | A range for ONE new value. Wider than a CI because it must cover one value's own spread too. | Mean plus or minus 1.96 x SD (95 percent); use 2.58 for 99 percent. Example: mean 100, SD 10 gives 80.4 to 119.6. | "Range for the next value" | Using SE here. One new value uses SD, not SE. |
| Confidence interval for the mean (CI) | A range for the TRUE MEAN. Not for one new value. 95 percent of intervals built this way catch the true mean. | Sample mean plus or minus multiplier x SE. Multiplier 1.96 at 95 percent, 2.58 at 99 percent. Small sample (N of 30 or less): use a t number, df = N minus 1. Example: N = 5, df = 4, t = 2.776, CI gets wider. | "Range for the true mean" | Building a CI when the question asks about one new value. That is a prediction interval, and it is wider. |
| Z-score | A distance. How far a value is from the middle, counted in spreads (SD units), not points. | z = (value minus mean) / SD. Example: (86 minus 80) / 4 = 1.5. Plus means above, minus means below. | "How far from the mean is this value?" | Miss log: confusing a z-score with the 95 percent cutoff. 1.96 is a cutoff picked to catch 95 percent. It is not the definition of a z-score. Status in miss log: revisit later, unanswered. |
| Interval cutoff (1.96, 2.58) | A chosen number of spreads, picked to catch a set share: 1.96 for 95 percent, 2.58 for 99 percent. | Used as the multiplier in PI and CI formulas above. | "Build a 95 percent (or 99 percent) range" | Miss log: treating 1.96 as if it were computed from the data like a z-score. It is picked, not computed. |
| Law of Large Numbers (LLN) | As the sample gets big, the sample mean gets close to the true mean. | No formula in held notes: not in held notes, verify if a formula is needed. | "What happens to the sample mean as N grows?" | Miss log: CLT vs LLN was missed Oct 25, then a clean retest pass Oct 26. LLN is about one growing sample closing in. It is not the bell shape claim. |
| Central Limit Theorem (CLT) | Averages act normal, even when the raw data do not. Sample means pile up in a bell shape centred on the true mean. This is why CIs for means work with big samples. | No formula in held notes beyond: sampling distribution of means is centred on the true mean, spread is SE. | "Distribution of sample means" or "why can we build a CI for the mean?" | Small samples from very non-normal data: be careful, per notes. Also swapping CLT with LLN (see LLN row). |
| Reject H0 | The decision when p is below the 0.05 cutoff. Enough proof against the claim. | Test step first: t = (sample mean minus claimed mean) / SE, then read p. | p below 0.05 in a testing question | Miss log, REPEAT miss: p = 0.04 (Oct 25) and p = 0.012 (Oct 26) both answered "fail to reject". Rule: small p means reject. p under 0.05 rejects no matter how the option words it. |
| Fail to reject H0 | The decision when p is at or above 0.05. Not enough proof. It does NOT prove the claim true. | Same test skeleton as above. Class example: t = 0.617, p = 0.537, do not reject. The claim 0.5 also sat inside the 95 percent CI (0.266 to 0.948), same story. | p at or above 0.05 in a testing question | Reading "fail to reject" as "the claim is true", or reading p as the chance the claim is true. Notes say both are wrong: p is how often a gap this big would appear by chance IF the claim were true. |
| One-tailed vs two-tailed test | Two-tailed: true mean is not equal to the claim, either side counts. One-tailed: true mean is above the claim, or below it, one side only. | H0 is the claim, H1 is the other side. Class examples: two-tailed gain claim 0.5 gave p = 0.537, do not reject. One-tailed "above 0.25" gave t = 2.055, p = 0.020, reject. | Wording "differs from" (two-tailed) vs "above" or "below" (one-tailed) | Using a two-tailed reading when the question asks only about one direction, or the reverse. Not a recorded miss: trap stated from the notes' own split of the two Netflix examples. |
